# Decision Making Tradition

| **Tradition & Era**                      | **Core Assumption**                                         | **Best Used When**                                       | **Overuse Risk**                                        | **Example**                                            |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Rational / Analytical** _(1900–1930s)_ | Problems are structurable; options are directly comparable. | Objectives and data are clear and reliable.              | False precision; ignores politics and model limits.     | Capital budgeting & equipment replacement              |
| **Behavioral / Bounded** _(1940–1980s)_  | Decision makers have cognitive and information limits.      | Time, attention, and data are scarce.                    | Premature satisficing; stopping search too early.       | Vendor selection under tight deadlines                 |
| **Political / Coalitional**              | Choices reflect power, self-interest, and negotiation.      | Stakeholders have conflicting objectives.                | Cynicism; bargaining without solving core issues.       | Cross-functional resource allocation                   |
| **Intuitive / Expert** _(Pre-1900s)_     | Experts use rapid pattern recognition from practice.        | High-speed decisions by experienced leaders.             | Overconfidence; forcing outdated patterns on new cases. | Trader or plant manager spotting anomalies             |
| **AI / Algorithmic** _(1990–2000s)_      | Quantitative models improve prediction and optimization.    | Large datasets with repeated, measurable choices.        | Automation bias; optimizing metrics over true goals.    | Dynamic pricing & inventory optimization               |
| **Integrative** _(2000–Present)_         | Opposing models can be reconciled and synthesized.          | Existing choices fail and trade-offs are not inevitable. | Endless synthesis loops without making decisions.       | Hybrid business model (e.g., low-cost + high intimacy) |
